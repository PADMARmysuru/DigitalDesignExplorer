Built-in self-test (BIST)

External testers are expensive and cannot easily reach deeply embedded blocks at full speed. Built-in self-test puts a small tester inside the chip. The chip generates its own patterns, applies them at its own clock speed, compresses the responses and reports a single pass/fail.

BIST is used for memories (MBIST) and logic (LBIST), for at-speed testing, and for testing chips in the field, for example every time a car's electronics power up.

3

LFSR: the pattern generator

A linear feedback shift register (LFSR) is a shift register whose input is the XOR of some of its bits (the taps). It needs only flip-flops and XOR gates, yet produces a long, random-looking sequence.

With the right taps, an n-bit LFSR visits every non-zero state before repeating: a maximal-length sequence of 2n − 1 patterns. Try different taps on this 4-bit LFSR and find which give all 15 states.

The all-zero state is a trap: XOR of zeros is zero, so the LFSR would stay at 0000 forever. That is why the seed must be non-zero.
4

Signature analysis

Storing every expected response on chip would take too much memory. Instead, the response stream is fed into another LFSR, a signature register, which compresses it into a short signature. Only the final signature is compared with the expected (golden) one. A register with several parallel inputs is called a MISR (multiple-input signature register).

Tap bits in the faulty response stream to create errors, and compare the two signatures.

Compression loses information. Occasionally a faulty stream produces the good signature, called aliasing. For an n-bit signature register the aliasing probability is about 2−n: 1 in 16 here, but about 1 in 65,536 for a 16-bit register.
5

BILBO: one register, four jobs

A BILBO (built-in logic block observer) register can switch between four modes using two control bits, B1 and B2. The same flip-flops act as normal registers, a scan chain, a pattern generator or a signature analyser, so no separate test registers are needed.

Boundary scan (IEEE 1149.1, JTAG)

On a modern board, chips in ball-grid packages have their pins hidden underneath, so a tester cannot touch them with probes. Boundary scan places a small scan cell between the core and every pin. All the cells form one chain that is controlled through four pins: TDI (data in), TDO (data out), TCK (clock) and TMS (mode select), plus an optional TRST.

With the EXTEST instruction, chip 1's output cells drive the board wires and chip 2's input cells capture them. Inject a board defect and apply test vectors.

7

The TAP controller

Every JTAG chip contains the same 16-state TAP controller. It changes state on each rising edge of TCK, depending on TMS. Two parallel paths do similar jobs: the DR path works on data registers, the IR path on the instruction register. Holding TMS = 1 for five clocks always returns to Test-Logic-Reset.

Choose a goal, then set TMS and pulse TCK to walk there.

What you learned

  • BIST adds a pattern generator, a response analyser and a controller on chip, enabling at-speed and in-field testing.
  • An n-bit LFSR with suitable taps generates 2n − 1 pseudo-random patterns and must never start at all-zero.
  • Signature registers compress long responses into a short signature, with a small aliasing risk of about 2−n.
  • A BILBO register can work as a normal register, scan chain, pattern generator or signature analyser.
  • Boundary scan puts a scan cell at every pin so board wiring can be tested without physical probes.
  • The TAP controller is a 16-state machine driven by TMS on each TCK edge; five TMS = 1 clocks always reset it.
  • EXTEST tests board interconnects, BYPASS shortens the chain, SAMPLE/PRELOAD captures pin values and IDCODE reads the chip ID.
